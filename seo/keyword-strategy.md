# Banana Hacks — Keyword Strategy & Map

> **On the difficulty numbers below:** they're informed estimates based on SERP
> composition, not pulled from Ahrefs/Semrush. Verify before committing budget.
> How to check for free: search the term in an incognito window and count how
> many of the top 10 are (a) major platforms like Devpost/MLH, (b) news, or
> (c) small sites. Three or more small sites in the top 10 = winnable.

---

## 1. Competitors

Two very different sets, and they need different responses.

### Direct — other AI hackathons competing for the same registrants

| Competitor | What they rank for | Why they win |
|---|---|---|
| **lablab.ai** | "ai hackathon", "generative ai hackathon" | Runs events continuously; huge volume of event pages; strong domain |
| **Devpost** | almost every "\<topic\> hackathon" query | Marketplace with thousands of indexed event pages |
| **Major League Hacking (mlh.io)** | "hackathon 2026", "virtual hackathon" | Authority + the official season calendar |
| **Devfolio** | "online hackathon", India-heavy queries | Platform effect, strong backlinks |
| **HackClub** | "high school hackathon" | Community + .edu/.org backlinks |

**Read:** you will not out-rank platforms on head terms like "ai hackathon".
Don't try. They win on domain authority and page count, both of which take
years. Compete where they're weak: **specific, intent-heavy long-tail** and
**your own brand**.

### Indirect — where your audience actually is
Reddit (r/StableDiffusion, r/MachineLearning), Hugging Face forums, X/Twitter
AI-art circles, Discord servers. These don't rank against you; they're
**distribution**, and they're covered in the off-site playbook.

### Cannibalisation check
The risk here is `/about` and `/` both chasing "generative ai hackathon". They
are deliberately split: `/` targets the **brand** term and carries the event
schema; `/about` targets the **category** term. Keep it that way — if you later
add a blog post about generative AI hackathons, point it at `/about` with
anchor text "generative AI hackathon" rather than letting it compete.

## 3. Long-tail targets (where the actual wins are)

These have low volume individually but convert far better and are genuinely
winnable. Each is already answered on the site — the job now is to make sure
the answer is *the best one on the internet*.

**Question-shaped (already in FAQPage schema, eligible for People Also Ask):**
- "can beginners do an AI hackathon"
- "do you need a team for a hackathon"
- "can you use pre-trained models in a hackathon"
- "what do you submit to a hackathon"
- "are online hackathons free"
- "how are hackathon projects judged"
- "what can you build with stable diffusion in a weekend"

**Comparison / discovery-shaped (not yet covered — see gaps below):**
- "best AI hackathons 2026"
- "virtual hackathons for beginners"
- "hackathons with compute credits"
- "generative AI competitions"

### Content gaps worth filling
The site now covers every *event* query. It covers no *topic* queries, which is
where the recurring, non-seasonal traffic lives. Highest-value additions, in
order:

1. **"What to build at a generative AI hackathon" — 15 project ideas.**
   Ranks year-round, links naturally to `/register`, and is genuinely useful to
   your registrants. Best effort-to-return ratio on this list.
2. **"Stable Diffusion in a weekend: a beginner's guide."** Targets the
   workshop topic; can be repurposed as pre-event material.
3. **"How to win a hackathon (from a judge's perspective)."** Highly shareable,
   attracts links from other hackathon organisers.

A caveat worth stating plainly: content marketing pays off over 3–6 months, and
your event is in October 2026. If you only have bandwidth for one thing, the
off-site playbook will move registrations faster than blog posts will.

## 4. Intent analysis of what currently ranks

Search "generative ai hackathon" and the top 10 is roughly:
- 6–7 **platform listing pages** (lablab.ai, Devpost) — aggregators, not events
- 2–3 **individual event pages**
- 0–1 informational articles

**What that tells you:** Google reads this query as *"show me a list of
hackathons I can join"*, not *"explain hackathons to me"*. That's why `/about`
leads with concrete details (dates, cost, eligibility) rather than an essay —
it matches the intent that's actually ranking. It also tells you that getting
**listed on the aggregators is worth more than out-ranking them**, which is
exactly what the off-site playbook prioritises.
